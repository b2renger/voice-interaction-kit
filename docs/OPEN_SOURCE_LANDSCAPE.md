# No open-source project replaces your kit

No: as of October 2026, no open-source project already does what your "voice interaction" kit does. Several projects cover one or two pieces of it, but none combines all four of its ingredients: a cloud engine and a local engine behind one shared state vocabulary, settings designed for designers (persona, voice, end-of-turn silence, interruption, delay, earcons), visible calls to action, and a test protocol with exercises built for a design school. Five projects come closest. **Pipecat** is the most complete technically, but it is a Python framework for developers. **LiveKit Agents** has the most explicit state vocabulary. **dotsimulate's LOPs for TouchDesigner** are the most direct creative-coding equivalent, but they are distributed through Patreon with no public license. **Micdrop** has the closest architecture (TypeScript, WebSocket, Gemini Live plus local models). **VoiceIt from TU Delft** is the only tool built for a design course, but it uses no live model. We found no public cables.gl op connected to Gemini Live or any LLM, so your VoiceAgent op appears to be the first. The recommended strategy is therefore to keep your foundation and borrow specific parts. The top priorities are Smart Turn v3.2 (BSD-2, French-capable, 8 MB on CPU) alongside your silence setting, LiveKit's state names, a "thinking sound" bound to the thinking state, Pipecat's UI channel for calls to action, and the latency × filler experimental protocol published at CUI '25. Five findings affect your current choices: Supertonic has been archived since September 9, 2026; QVAC shipped four breaking releases between late August and early October 2026; the licenses of LiveKit's and TEN's end-of-turn models rule them out; Gemini Live reportedly cannot use custom-designed voices (a single report, to be confirmed); and no one has publicly measured the local engine's latency on a Windows laptop without a GPU.

## Five projects come close, none covers the kit

The projects fall into four families, and each covers only part of your requirements: developer frameworks (Pipecat, LiveKit), creative-coding integrations (LOPs for TouchDesigner), local voice apps (HF speech-to-speech, Open-LLM-VTuber, AIRI, Unmute) and teaching tools (VoiceIt, MIT App Inventor). None of them links real-time engineering to design teaching.

**Pipecat is the most complete technical equivalent.** This Python framework from Daily is BSD-2 licensed, has 16.3k stars and released version 1.12.0 on September 26, 2026. It puts cloud speech-to-speech services, including **Gemini Multimodal Live** and OpenAI Realtime, in the same pipeline as local components such as Whisper, Ollama, Kokoro or Piper ([GitHub Pipecat](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pipecat-ai/)). Its open client protocol, RTVI, emits events very close to yours: user and bot start/stop speaking, LLM start, function-call lifecycle, metrics ([Pipecat RTVI](https://docs.pipecat.ai/client/rtvi-standard)). But it is a developer tool with no visual editor: "You write the orchestration in Python; no drag-and-drop builder" ([Techsy](https://techsy.io/en/blog/best-open-source-voice-agent-frameworks)). It requires Python 3.11, native Windows support is not documented, and its structured-dialogue layer, Pipecat Flows, **does not support speech-to-speech models such as Gemini Live** ([Pipecat Flows](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat-flows/introduction.md)). To get your kit, you would have to rebuild everything on top of it.

**LiveKit Agents has the most explicit state vocabulary.** Apache-2.0 licensed, with 13k stars and version 1.8.6 released on October 9, 2026, it publishes an agent state of `initializing / idle / listening / thinking / speaking` to the interface ([LiveKit events](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/events/)). It supports Gemini Live and Ollama but assumes a LiveKit server; asked about offline use in January 2026, a community member answered "Not offline, no" ([LiveKit community](https://community.livekit.io/t/is-it-possible-to-use-offline-stt-whisper-and-tts-with-livekit/188)). Its no-code Agent Builder deploys only to LiveKit Cloud ([LiveKit Agent Builder](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/start/builder)).

**dotsimulate's LOPs are your closest creative-coding cousin.** Version 0.3.0 (May 2026) gave TouchDesigner a unified "Voice Agent" operator (Gemini Live, OpenAI Realtime, Grok Voice, Hume EVI; not to be confused with your own VoiceAgent op), a "Voice Activity" operator with echo cancellation, Silero VAD and **Smart Turn v3 end-of-turn detection**, and local Kokoro and Pocket TTS voices ([LOPs changelog](https://docs.dotsimulate.com/changelog/)). That is exactly the architecture of your op: a provider-agnostic operator, a separate turn-taking operator and swappable local voices. But LOPs are distributed through Patreon, their license is not published, and our sources mention no documented state vocabulary and no teaching material.

**Micdrop is the closest in architecture.** This MIT-licensed TypeScript library (browser client, Node server, WebSocket, voice detection in the browser) handles Gemini Live and OpenAI Realtime on one side and Whisper, Kokoro, Piper, Pocket TTS and Qwen3-TTS on the other, with Ollama, LM Studio or llama.cpp as the LLM ([micdrop.dev](https://micdrop.dev/); [Micdrop local models](https://micdrop.dev/docs/ai-integration/local-models)). Its exposed state is minimal (a `StateChange` event with `isAssistantSpeaking`), it has a single maintainer, and it offers no interface for designers ([Micdrop blog](https://micdrop.dev/blog/open-source-voice-agent-frameworks)).

**On the local side, no app exposes settings comparable to yours.** HF speech-to-speech (Apache-2.0, 11k stars) offers a fully local mode on Apple Silicon and an OpenAI-Realtime-compatible server, but it is Python and has no design interface ([GitHub HF s2s](https://github.com/huggingface/speech-to-speech)). Open-LLM-VTuber (MIT, 13.5k stars) runs offline on Windows, macOS and Linux with a Live2D avatar and headphone-free interruption, but its v1 now receives only bug fixes ([GitHub Open-LLM-VTuber](https://github.com/Open-LLM-VTuber/Open-LLM-VTuber)). AIRI (MIT, 49.9k stars) shows that an Electron voice app can ship as a Windows .exe and a macOS arm64 .dmg, while describing itself as "still in the early stage of development" ([GitHub AIRI](https://github.com/moeru-ai/airi)). Kyutai Unmute, from a Paris lab, defines its characters in a `voices.yaml` file (voice, instructions, language en/fr), but it requires a 16 GB CUDA GPU, excludes macOS and has no tool calling ([GitHub Unmute](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute)). Mainstream tools do less than you: Open WebUI's voice mode sends the message after "about two seconds of silence" with no documented setting, and interruption is off by default ([Open WebUI](https://docs.openwebui.com/features/chat-conversations/chat-features/voice-mode)). QVAC's official voice-assistant recipe has neither interruption nor French (see below).

**On the teaching side, VoiceIt is the only peer, and it complements your kit rather than competing with it.** Gerd Kortuem built it for TU Delft's IDEM307 course (MIT, v0.5.1, "in development", 1 star). It plays scripted scenarios with a character, a voice and `SOUND`, `LIGHT`, `SCREEN` and `(pause N)` lines, with no live model and no turn-taking setting, and it has been tested only in Chrome on macOS ([GitHub VoiceIt](https://github.com/kortuem/voiceit)). VoiceIt is for storyboarding behavior before a model is plugged in; your kit brings the real behavior to life (latency, interruptions, misunderstanding). Everything else is dated: the MIT App Inventor curriculum relies on Alexa ([MIT App Inventor](https://appinventor.mit.edu/explore/convo-ai)), and the Google AIY Voice Kit software has been archived since February 2023 ([GitHub AIY](https://github.com/google/aiyprojects-raspbian)). In cables.gl, the only official voice op relies on Web Speech recognition, which "will send recorded data to google servers" ([cables.gl](https://cables.gl/op/Ops.Extension.Voice.SpeechRecognition)), and its example patch does not start because of an invalid language code (bug #8741, opened October 8, 2026) ([GitHub cables](https://github.com/cables-gl/cables/issues/8741)). Searches found no cables op or patch for Gemini Live, OpenAI Realtime or any LLM, but the discourse.cables.gl forum could not be searched.

## Your state vocabulary already matches the industry's

The industry kits have converged on roughly the same states, which validates your choice. LiveKit separates an agent state (`initializing, idle, listening, thinking, speaking`) from a user state (`speaking, listening, away`), with the user switching to `away` after 15 s of inactivity by default ([LiveKit events](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/events/)); its UI components add `disconnected, connecting, pre-connect-buffering, failed` ([useAgent.ts](https://github.com/livekit/components-js/blob/HEAD/packages/react/src/hooks/useAgent.ts)). The ElevenLabs Orb accepts `thinking | listening | talking` ([orb.tsx](https://github.com/elevenlabs/ui/blob/HEAD/apps/www/registry/elevenlabs-ui/ui/orb.tsx)), OpenAI's voice widget uses `busy | connecting | error | idle | listening | live` ([realtime-voice-component](https://github.com/openai/realtime-voice-component)), and Home Assistant emits `stt-vad-start`, `stt-vad-end`, `intent-progress` and `error` ([Home Assistant](https://developers.home-assistant.io/docs/voice/pipelines/)).

On one point, LiveKit models things more precisely than your kit: it treats "the user is speaking" as a user state, separate from the agent's state. Your "hearing" state therefore corresponds to "agent listening + user speaking". Keep your four names for students, but publish a mapping table like this one in the documentation, so they can read other kits' docs without getting lost.

| Your kit | LiveKit | Pipecat RTVI | ElevenLabs Orb | Home Assistant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| listening | agent `listening` | between `bot-stopped-speaking` and `user-started-speaking` | `listening` | `stt-start` |
| hearing | agent `listening` + user `speaking` | `user-started-speaking` → `user-stopped-speaking` | (input volume) | `stt-vad-start` → `stt-vad-end` |
| thinking | `thinking` | `bot-llm-started`, `llm-function-call-*` | `thinking` | `intent-start`, `intent-progress` |
| speaking | `speaking` | `bot-started-speaking` → `bot-stopped-speaking` | `talking` | `tts-start` → `tts-end` |
| *to add* | `initializing`, `idle`, `away`, `failed` | `bot-ready`, `error` | — | `error`, `run-end` |

*Proposed mapping based on the cited event lists; the columns do not always line up exactly.*

These kits also handle edge cases your vocabulary may be missing, and each one can become a design exercise. Start-up comes first: `connecting`, `failed` and especially `pre-connect-buffering`, where the microphone is already capturing the user before the session is ready, a real situation when a Gemini Live session opens. Then prolonged silence (`idle`, `away`): the W3C NAUR accessibility requirements ask for no unnecessary time-outs, or adjustable ones with a warning (REQ 20) ([W3C NAUR](https://w3c.github.io/naur/)). False interruption: LiveKit emits `agent_false_interruption` with a `resumed` field, so the agent picks its sentence back up when the "interruption" was only an "uh-huh" or a cough. A request to stop: TEN's model classifies user speech as *finished / unfinished / wait*, where "wait" means the user wants the agent to be quiet, an intent distinct from "I'm done" ([HF TEN](https://huggingface.co/TEN-framework/TEN_Turn_Detection)). A tool that is still running: LiveKit had to fix agent and user states while a tool runs in 1.7.1 ([LiveKit releases](https://github.com/livekit/agents/releases)), a sign that this case causes real problems. Finally, mood: LiveKit's beta "Expressive Mode" reduces mood to 11 values that fade after two turns, "so a feeling doesn't outlive the moment that produced it" ([useAgentExpression.ts](https://github.com/livekit/components-js/blob/HEAD/packages/react/src/hooks/useAgentExpression.ts)), a good model for linking persona to the color of a visual.

For calls to action, three models are worth studying. **RTVI's UI channel** sends `ui-command` messages from the server (scroll, highlight, click, set input, toast, navigate), receives a `ui-snapshot` from the client containing the screen's accessibility tree, and receives `ui-event` messages describing user actions ([Pipecat RTVI](https://docs.pipecat.ai/client/rtvi-standard)); our sources do not say whether it works with a Gemini Live pipeline. OpenAI's **realtime-voice-component** (Apache-2.0), presented as a reference implementation "useful for education, demos", exposes narrow, typed app tools (`defineVoiceTool`) and a "ghost cursor" that makes the agent's action visible as it happens ([GitHub OpenAI](https://github.com/openai/realtime-voice-component)). The OpenAI Agents SDK's **`tool_approval_required`** event holds a tool until it is approved ([OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/realtime/guide/)), which is exactly what NAUR requires before any irreversible action. In practice: treat an on-screen choice as a narrow, typed tool, show the action before executing it, and ask for confirmation on anything that cannot be undone.

**Borrowing #4 deserves top teaching priority, because its result contradicts a design reflex.** The CUI '25 study varied the reply delay (1.5 / 4 / 6.5 s) and the type of filler with 54 participants: none, a processing icon with a sound, or a thinking gesture with a "Hmm, let's see…". Beyond 4 s, quality of experience drops; natural fillers clearly improve perceived response time, while the icon and sound do not significantly improve overall perception ([arXiv 2507.22352](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22352v1)). Borrowing #1 also lends itself to a comparative exercise, since there are four ways to decide that a turn is over. Silence, as in your current setting; a prosodic audio model, such as Smart Turn; semantic detection built into transcription, such as Kyutai's `stt-1b-en_fr` model ([Kyutai DSM](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/delayed-streams-modeling)); or asking the LLM whether the sentence is complete, as Micdrop does ([Micdrop blog](https://micdrop.dev/blog/open-source-voice-agent-frameworks)). The first two can be built into Voice Lab; the other two work as discussed counter-examples. For a **voice identity** exercise, Qwen3-TTS VoiceDesign (Apache-2.0, supports French) generates a voice from a written description, so students can iterate on the description the way they would on a persona sheet ([GitHub Qwen3-TTS](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS)). We do not know whether the 1.7B model runs usably on a student laptop: test it on a studio machine first.

### Supertonic is archived: keep it this term, plan its replacement

The Voice Builder has been unavailable since August 31, and the repository has been **archived since September 9, 2026**, with the weights still on Hugging Face ([supertone-oss-archive](https://github.com/supertone-oss-archive/supertonic)). Your GGUF files will keep working in QVAC, but there will be no more fixes or new voices, and Supertonic 3's zero-shot voice styles, which depended on the Voice Builder, went with it ([HF Supertonic 3](https://huggingface.co/Supertone/supertonic-3)). One point needs checking. Supertonic 2 supports French (en/ko/es/pt/fr) under an OpenRAIL-M license that carries use restrictions ([HF Supertonic 2](https://huggingface.co/Supertone/supertonic-2)), but QVAC's default constant is `TTS_EN_SUPERTONIC_Q8_0`, labeled "Supertonic2 (English)" ([QVAC voice assistant](https://docs.qvac.tether.io/ai-capabilities/voice-assistant/)), and our sources do not confirm that the GGUF QVAC ships covers French voices.

**Keep a local copy of your current GGUF files**, and prepare a plan B with **Chatterbox**, already built into QVAC with cloning from a WAV ([QVAC TTS](https://docs.qvac.tether.io/ai-capabilities/text-to-speech/)). Its Multilingual variant (MIT) covers more than 23 languages including French, and every output carries a Perth watermark ([GitHub Chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)); check that this is the variant QVAC ships. Don't count on Parler-TTS in QVAC for French, since its variants there are English or Indic. Outside QVAC, **Pocket TTS** has spoken French since May 2026, runs about 6× faster than real time on a MacBook Air M4 CPU and can clone voices ([GitHub Pocket TTS](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts)); its maintainers call the small French variant "not great yet", however, and the weights license is not stated ([issue #118](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts/issues/118)). Avoid Kokoro for French, which has a single French voice graded B- ([Kokoro VOICES](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/blob/main/VOICES.md)), and don't bundle Piper into a distributed app, since it moved to GPLv3 as of v1.3.0 ([piper1-gpl](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/releases)).

### QVAC fits, but only upgrade between terms

QVAC is genuinely open source (Apache-2.0), but its community is small: about 561 stars, 107 forks and an empty Releases section on GitHub ([GitHub QVAC](https://github.com/tetherto/qvac)). It shipped **four breaking releases in six weeks**: 0.18.0 on August 24, 0.19.0 on September 7, 0.20.0 on September 22 and 0.21.0 on October 4, 2026 ([QVAC changelog](https://qvac.tether.io/changelog)). On Hacker News, the team acknowledges "large bundle sizes" and a plugin system that needs simplifying ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47708697)). Its official voice-assistant recipe lags behind your Voice Lab: English only, Whisper tiny and Llama 3.2 1B by default, a fixed 700 ms end of turn, the microphone muted while the agent speaks plus a 300 ms cooldown, **no interruption**, `ffplay` required and no event API ([QVAC voice assistant](https://docs.qvac.tether.io/ai-capabilities/voice-assistant/)). The examples repository has no complete voice assistant either ([qvac-examples](https://github.com/tetherto/qvac-examples)): your app could become the community example the project is missing.

Don't switch foundations because of this. The other local stacks are Python and often built for CUDA, whereas QVAC is one of the few JavaScript SDKs that targets both Windows x64 and Mac arm64. **Pin the `@qvac/sdk` version for a whole term** and test Windows x64 and Mac M1 before each upgrade. **Pre-install the models** from a USB stick or a local network server: QVAC accepts a local path or an HTTP URL, and otherwise downloads models peer-to-peer over Holepunch and "blind relays" ([QVAC blind relays](https://qvac-docs-prod.kinsta.page/sdk/blind-relays)), whose behavior on a filtered school network is undocumented. Also test **Parakeet** for French transcription, since QVAC offers it alongside Whisper: Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3, under CC BY 4.0, reports roughly 5% word error rate in French ([HF Parakeet v3](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3)), but our sources do not say which Parakeet variant QVAC provides. The list of prebuilt Electron platforms is not documented either.

### For end of turn, only Smart Turn is legally clean

LiveKit's turn detector performs well and supports French, but its license forbids using it outside LiveKit Agents: "cannot use the LiveKit models on a standalone basis or with any other frameworks" ([LiveKit LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/livekit/turn-detector/blob/main/LICENSE)). That rules it out for both Voice Lab and VoiceAgent. TEN Turn Detection is out too: 7 billion parameters, English and Chinese only, and an "Apache 2.0 with additional restrictions" license ([GitHub TEN turn detection](https://github.com/TEN-framework/ten-turn-detection)). More importantly, the TEN framework license forbids hosting it "on any End User devices", which is incompatible with student laptops ([TEN LICENSE](https://github.com/TEN-framework/ten-framework/blob/main/LICENSE)). TEN VAD is attractive (a tiny library, a WASM build, faster than Silero at detecting the switch to silence), but it carries "additional conditions" of the same kind ([GitHub TEN VAD](https://github.com/TEN-framework/ten-vad)): have those conditions checked legally before using it.

**Smart Turn v3.2 is the only legally clean option.** It is BSD-2, with open data, training script and weights; it has about 8 million parameters (8 MB in int8 on CPU), covers 23 languages including French and responds in "as little as 10ms on some CPUs" ([GitHub Smart Turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)); LOPs already use it outside Pipecat. Three uncertainties remain: an older Pipecat documentation page still calls it "designed for English" ([Pipecat docs](https://docs.pipecat.ai/server/utilities/smart-turn/smart-turn-overview)), no French accuracy figures were found, and we have not confirmed that its file format runs in Node/Electron. Budget a one-day prototype before committing. Finally, no open-source, local backchannel detector exists today: LiveKit's adaptive interruption, which tells an "uh-huh" apart from a real interruption, requires LiveKit Cloud in production, and "Gemini Live isn't compatible" ([LiveKit adaptive interruption](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/adaptive-interruption-handling.md); [LiveKit community](https://community.livekit.io/t/roadmap-for-adaptive-interruption-handling-in-self-hosted-livekit-workers/1172)). Your interruption settings will therefore rest on simple rules (minimum duration, minimum word count, resume after a false interruption), which are easier for students to read anyway.

### Gemini Live limits voice identity and freezes during tool calls

On September 24, 2026, a developer reported that the Gemini 3.8 TTS Voice Design API works but that voices created with it "cannot currently be used with `gemini-3.8-live`", with no official reply visible ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-8-live-support-for-voice-design-custom-voices/184651)). This is a single source, to be confirmed against the official documentation. If it holds, **voice identity on the cloud side is limited to preset voices and the style requested in the prompt**, and designed or cloned voices exist only in the local engine: a contrast worth turning into course material rather than treating as a defect.

According to LiveKit's integration docs, `gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview` does not support asynchronous function calling, so the model pauses until the tool returns ([LiveKit Gemini](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/integrations/realtime/gemini)). We only read this limitation in LiveKit's docs; check it for the model you use. If it applies, every tool call creates a silence that VoiceAgent must cover explicitly with a "tool running" state, in sound or visuals. Pipecat also notes that Gemini Live "don't emit" its own speaking signals and therefore keeps voice detection and Smart Turn running locally ([Pipecat releases](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/releases)); we infer, without having checked it against your op, that the "hearing" state would be more responsive if computed in the browser (borrowing #12). The Live API is still in "Preview" on Firebase, with `gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview` and `gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-preview` as the listed models ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/live-api?hl=en)): expect changes from one term to the next. It advertises 70 languages, barge-in, tool use and transcriptions ([gemini-live-api-examples](https://github.com/google-gemini/gemini-live-api-examples)), and Full-Duplex-Bench has already measured it on pause handling, backchannels and interruption ([arXiv 2503.04721](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04721v2)), so students can place their bots against those measurements.

### Local latency is known on Mac, unknown on Windows without a GPU

Micdrop publishes measurements on a MacBook Pro M2 with models preloaded ([Micdrop performance](https://micdrop.dev/docs/ai-integration/local-models/performance.md)). Whisper base transcribes a 3 s sentence in about 440 ms, versus about 1,100 ms for a French Whisper checkpoint; Qwen3 4B produces its first token in 100 to 150 ms; the first synthesized sentence arrives in 250 to 430 ms with Pocket TTS, about 390 ms with Piper and 900 to 1,300 ms with Kokoro. End to end, Qwen3 4B with French Whisper and Piper gives roughly **1.6 s to first word**, and the CoreML Whisper encoder turned out slower than the CPU one. These figures put a local engine on an M1/M2 Mac well below the 4 s threshold identified by CUI '25.

For Windows x64 without a GPU, no measurement was found; the only estimate, 4 to 6 s on CPU alone, comes from a competing vendor and should be treated with caution ([InnerZero](https://innerzero.com/blog/best-local-private-voice-ai-assistant-2026)). QVAC can offload synthesis to the GPU through Vulkan on Windows ([QVAC TTS](https://docs.qvac.tether.io/ai-capabilities/text-to-speech/)), which might help on integrated GPUs, but nothing quantifies it. If the estimate holds, Windows laptops would cross the threshold where thinking feedback stops being a detail and becomes a necessity. **Measure on the school's actual machines**: that mini-benchmark could itself become the module's first exercise.

## Conclusion

What open source is missing is not the technology but its translation for designers. The industry has converged on the same states and the same settings as you (end-of-turn silence, interruption threshold, thinking sound), but those settings live in Python configuration files written for engineers. Your kit's value lies in turning them into design parameters and pairing them with a test protocol. The priority is therefore not to add engine features but to document the mapping to industry vocabulary and publish what already exists: VoiceAgent would be the first public cables.gl op for Gemini Live, and Voice Lab already outperforms QVAC's official recipe.

The risk sits in the local engine's dependencies (Supertonic archived, QVAC unstable at 0.x, Windows latency unknown) rather than in the concept; on the cloud side, it lies in Google's "Preview" status and its fixed voices. Keeping the designer-facing vocabulary clearly separate from the underlying wire protocol protects your exercises: you will be able to swap a voice, a turn detector or even an entire engine (Pipecat or HF speech-to-speech) without rewriting a single course handout.
